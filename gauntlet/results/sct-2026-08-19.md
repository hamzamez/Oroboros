# Size-change termination, and what it does to the interval residue

2026-08-19. Lee, Jones & Ben-Amram, *The size-change principle for program termination* (POPL
2001), built and measured — [types-direction.md §6.6](../../docs/spec/../types-direction.md)
recommended it as the cheapest way to close two holes at once.

## The two holes

**[intervals-2026-08-19](intervals-2026-08-19.md)** found that the entire unproven residue was one
class: *a loop variable bounded by the trip count rather than by a guard on itself.* An accumulator
counting primes; a digit index whose guard is on the quotient.

**[concerns.md §2.1](../../docs/spec/concerns.md)** records that our termination guard is "a
*mechanism*, not a proof, and the fuel limit is an admission that the mechanism is incomplete". And
[ADR 0015](../../docs/decisions/0015-loop-and-again.md) declares that termination is "a computed
program property" — which nothing computed.

A trip count is a termination argument with a number attached. **They are the same hole from two
sides.**

## The result

| | ops proven | | loops terminating | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | **before** | **after** | **before** | **after** |
| nothing declared | 39% | **54%** | — | **54%** |
| one range declared | 81% | **100%** | — | **96%** |

Ten programs, 80 integer operations, 24 loops. `cmd/intervals`.

**Every integer operation in every program is provably inside the portable window once one range is
declared, and every loop but one is proven to terminate.**

The one exception is a true negative and the right answer: `examples/converge.oro` is Newton's
method on a **float**, looping until `|g² − x| < ε`. Its termination is a theorem about numerical
convergence, not a size-change argument, and the analysis correctly declines to claim it.

## What was built

`emit/sct.go` — the principle as stated: a graph per back edge with arcs labelled *strictly
decreases* or *does not increase*, closed under composition, and the criterion that **every
idempotent graph carries a strict self-arc**. Idempotent graphs are exactly the repeatable cycles.

Its power over "find a variable that always decreases" is that different variables may descend on
different paths, and the test suite includes that case: two edges where neither variable descends
on its own diagonal, which a naive check refuses and the composition accepts.

**Two things had to be added, and both are ours rather than the paper's.**

**A floor.** Classical size change assumes a well-founded order — structural descent on data. Our
loop variables are integers, and `x → x−1` runs forever without a bound below. So the descent is
only an argument when the interval analysis also supplies the floor, and the two passes are joined:
**SCT provides the descent, intervals provide the well-foundedness.** The floor is demanded of the
*witness*, not of every variable — a loop counting into an unbounded accumulator still terminates,
because the accumulator is not what carries the argument.

**Orientation.** A counter that *ascends* toward a ceiling is the same argument under μ = −v, which
is what a linear ranking function is. Doing it as a change of variable rather than a special case
keeps one algorithm for both directions.

**And then the number.** SCT proves a loop stops; the residue needed a *bound*. A measure that
descends by at least δ from a bounded range gives a trip count, and then every other variable is
`v₀ + T · step` — which is what finally bounds an accumulator no guard in the program mentions.
Geometric descent is handled too, because `m / 10` is exactly the decimal printer's loop.

This is strictly stronger than what the criterion alone gives, and strictly weaker in reach: **SCT
proves termination in cases we cannot number** — the alternating case above terminates and has no
single descending measure. That gap is real and is recorded rather than papered over.

## Three bugs found, and one of them was the whole residue

Worth recording, because two of the three made the analysis report *worse* numbers than the truth,
and the third would have made the result meaningless.

**A nested loop appended its back edge to its parent's list.** The collector is per-loop state on a
shared pass, and the parent's fixpoint runs before the child's state is installed — so the child's
`again` was recorded against the *parent's* variable names. Every arc came out empty and the parent
was reported as possibly non-terminating on a cycle nothing was known about. Five edges where the
loop has two.

**An `again` branch was joined into the loop's value as ⊤.** A back edge produces no value; joining
it as "anything" made *every loop with a back edge return anything*. **This was the entire remaining
residue.** The decimal printer's `m` starts at the count of primes, which the counting loop bounds
perfectly, and the bound was discarded on the way out because the loop's value was joined with the
branches that do not produce one. Fixing it took the windows sieve from 84% to 100%.

**Division threw away the sign.** `a / b` was bounded by `±|a|` regardless of signs, so `m / 10`
lost `m`'s floor — and with no floor there is no well-founded descent.

All three made numbers worse, which is the only reason they were not mistaken for results. That is
the same lesson as the first interval run and the retracted loop encoding: **an implementation's
weakness reads as a language's limit, and the way to tell them apart is to look at *why* each case
failed rather than at the percentage.**

## Soundness

`emit/sct_test.go` and `emit/interval_test.go`. The algebra is tested against non-increase alone
(must not prove), the alternating two-variable case (must prove), a cycle with no strict descent
(must not prove), and descent with no floor (must not prove). The interval pass must still fail to
bound a product of two 2³⁰ values, an accumulator over an unbounded loop, and an undeclared
parameter.

A change that raises the headline number by breaking one of those has measured nothing.

## What this does not settle

- **The corpus is small and it is all sieves.** Four programs carry almost every integer operation
  in the repository. 100% over a narrow corpus is a floor on confidence, not a ceiling.
- **Nothing is emitted differently yet.** This proves the information is *available*; using it to
  select a representation is the next step and a separate one.
- **The `-assume` knob is not syntax.** A range is writable today as
  `(sig f ((n int)) int (where …))`, and a library's precondition is checked at the definition but
  **dissolves when the definition is inlined** — whole-program reduction helps the analysis by
  turning parameters into literals, and hurts it by erasing the declaration that would have bounded
  them. Both directions of the same mechanism.
